The argumentSeven findings, in the order they build

Each depends on the one before it. Together they say: the pooled metric fails, we know why, and the metric that survives holds under a harder test.

  1. YES+PARTIAL is not a valid measure — random directions beat the SAE on it. At |r| 0.1–0.3, random scores 79% against the SAE's 58% (p = 6.4×10−5). A metric that noise wins is not measuring signal.
  2. The failure is localised to PARTIAL, not to the judge. exact-YES over the same features: SAE 0%, random 2%, p = 0.35. Same judge, same prompt, same features. Only the pooling differs.
  3. PARTIAL is a real category, not an artifact of offering it. Forced to choose, only 6–16% of PARTIALs become YES; the rest become NO. The judge was correctly declining to call clinically adjacent concepts a match.
  4. Stating the correlation inflates agreement about threefold. De-anchoring the prompt drops exact-YES from 37.5% to 12.9% at r>0.3. Our published number is anchored. This is the first measurement of that inflation.
  5. Hard negatives are what make the SAE result significant. Same-chapter distractors: JumpReLU +38.2pp (p = 0.003) versus +15.8pp and ns under cross-chapter. The easy slate let random directions win on organ-system plausibility alone.
  6. Label-supervised directions score below random. Diff-in-means 24%, probe LR 17%, random 38% at |r| 0.3–0.5 — despite both seeing the ICD labels and reaching |r| 0.70. Prediction and description come apart. This is what the SAE contributes.
  7. Two SAEs replicate; the general-purpose SAE does not compete. JumpReLU and ReLU+L1 agree within 3pp everywhere. GemmaScope has 13 features above r>0.4 against 276. Domain training, not architecture, is what matters.
